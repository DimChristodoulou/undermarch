Homebrew campaign bible · Draft 0.6

The UndermarchTwenty holes in the world, and a dark continent underneath it.

System Pathfinder 2e (Remaster) Setting Homebrew Sections 16 Tone Grim survival

01Core premise

Locked

Something tore twenty holes in the world. They opened onto a labyrinth beneath the crust so vast that no one has mapped a hundredth of it — a dark continent under everyone's feet, full of dead cities, unfamiliar ecologies, and things that have never seen a sky. The surface powers have descended, planted flags around each opening, and begun a gold rush into the dark.

None of them know the holes were made, deliberately, by something down there that wants up. An insectoid civilization of mages engineered them for access to the surface. If nobody stops them, they assemble an army and invade — within roughly one year.

Resolved — session 2

The insectoids made the Undertears themselves. There is no separate external entity — "external force" was shorthand for the fact that the world doesn't know they exist. The GM-side secret is simply who did this, and why. Original options archived below.

  • The insectoids are the agents; the external force is the fuel. They found, bargained with, or drilled into something and spent it. Cleanest — and it makes that force a resource players can contest. My recommendation.
  • The insectoids are the tools. Something used them; they believe they authored the Tears. Great rug-pull, but risks demoting your villain right when players finally care.
  • Two coincidental events. Weakest. Coincidence at this scale reads as mess, not mystery.

Resolved: the insectoids are the sole agents.

But there is something at the Root Locked

A mysterious entity is drawing heat out of the world from below. It did not make the Undertears and has no interest in the surface. It is the reason the deep is cooling, the reason the Stillness is failing, and therefore the ultimate reason the insectoids must migrate upward. It is the level 17–20 endgame.

One cause, two symptoms. The entity drains deep heat. Deep heat powers the Stillness. So it simultaneously freezes the insectoid homeland and dissolves its only protection — which is why the enchantment cannot simply be renewed. The fuel is being stolen faster than it can be gathered.

Open: what it is, whether it is awake, whether it is why the ways were sealed, and whether it can be reasoned with, fought, or only fled.

Elapsed time Locked

The Weeping was roughly two months ago. Ten months remain on the clock. This is a hard constraint and it rules out a great deal: no established colonies, no cable-cars or winch-towers, no surveyed routes, no deep-born population, no functioning trade, no completed dams.

Unresolved tension

Your brief said surface factions have "claimed their territories" and offer "relatively safe haven." At two months those havens are canvas, rope and mud.

  • (a) Keep two months and lean in. The colonies are camps. Descent is rope and improvised scaffold. Nobody has expertise; nobody has been below the Shallows. Dams are half-built and uneven, and only the wealthiest nations got one up — which splits the surface into nations that were saved and nations that were ruined, handing you a political map for free. Players get to be among the first to do almost anything, which quietly solves the "why hasn't someone stronger fixed this" problem. Recommended.
  • (b) Stretch to ~18 months. Real infrastructure, an established frontier economy, havens that feel safe. Costs the rawness, and you must explain why nobody noticed the thermal anomaly.

Use three names at once. The Weeping (folk), The Opening (charters and states), The Unroofing (deep-dwellers). Which name an NPC uses instantly tells the players who they are. Costs nothing, pays out every session.

The insectoids Blocked

Don't name them yet — their name depends on §05, specifically hive versus polity. Holding term: the Deep Choir.

03Structure of the deep

Critique — the scale trap

"A megadungeon spanning the entire planet" sounds enormous, and enormity is the enemy of prep, tone and stakes. If everything connects to everything, no journey means anything, no region feels distinct, and you owe your players a coherent geography the size of a planet. You will not build that. Nobody has.

The archipelago model Proposed

The Undermarch is not an ocean of tunnel. It's an archipelago — discrete deep regions, each with its own ecology and its own dead, separated by genuinely impassable gaps: flooded sumps, magma sheets, crushed dead zones, sheer voids, a hundred miles of unbroken rock.

  • Prep discipline. One region at a time, each self-contained. No obligation to connect them.
  • Tonal variety without incoherence. A fungal wetland and a frozen crystal vault aren't inconsistent — they're Iceland and Java.
  • It explains why the twenty Tears aren't linked below. Travel between them is easier on the surface. Delicious inversion.

The antagonist's real capability Proposed

Critique — power scaling

A faction that can tear a planet's crust in twenty places does not need infantry. If they can do the big thing, the small thing is beneath them, and your central threat collapses.

The fix, which also solves the archipelago: their defining capability isn't destruction, it's connection. They are the only power that can bore through the impassable gaps, and they have spent centuries stitching the archipelago into one navigable whole. Nobody else has this. Nobody else knows it exists.

  • The Undertears aren't their weapon — they're off-ramps. Twenty simultaneous breaches only matter if you can move an army between them.
  • The invasion becomes legible. You can draw the route. Players can cut it.
  • The ritual's cost is explicable. Boring twenty shafts at once bankrupted them. That's why they need soldiers now — they spent their magic on the doors.
  • It hands your players a transit system. Captured tunnels are the only fast route between distant Tears. The antagonist's greatest asset becomes your campaign's map.
  • It gives an escalating objective that isn't "kill the queen": find the network, map it, seize or collapse nodes.

Working names for the network: the Weftways / the Sutures / the Stitch.

Depth bands Proposed

PF2e's math is tight enough that you need a legible link between where you are and how hard it is. Depth is the cleanest axis: intuitive, physical, and players self-regulate against it.

BandDepthLevelCharacter
The Threshold0–1 km1–4Rope-and-ladder country. Colony infrastructure, surveyed routes, sunlight still reaches. Doline groves live here.
The Shallows1–4 km5–8Last of the surveyed world. Fungal forests, first ruined cities, competing charter expeditions.
The Mid Reaches4–12 km9–12Beyond any map. The great dead civilizations. First Weftway nodes.

The inverted gradient Locked

The Undermarch warms with depth exactly as it should — until somewhere in the Mid Reaches, where the gradient reverses. Below that it gets colder the deeper you go. The Root is the coldest place in the world. Cause: the entity at the Root is drawing the heat out.

Real-world anchorFigure
Normal geothermal gradient~25–30°C per km
Deepest mine (Mponeng)4 km; rock face ~66°C; cooled with pumped slurry ice
Deepest borehole (Kola)12.2 km; abandoned at ~180°C, rock gone plastic
Continental crust~30–50 km thick
  • Heat becomes the scarce resource, not the hazard. Civilizations huddle around thermal vents like campfires. When mapping you stop asking "where are the caves" and start asking "where is the heat" — which generates your settlement pattern for you. Dead cities are found where the heat went out.
  • It is a measurable anomaly. A party with a thermometer and a rope can discover the world is wrong. Falsifiable, physical, found rather than told.
  • It gives the antagonists a thermodynamic motive. They are not conquering, they are evacuating toward the only remaining heat source — a star.
  • It fills the Root. Endgame, sealing mystery and insectoid motive become one invention.

Rejected: conventional heat-as-hazard. Cleaner mechanically, but a hazard rather than an idea, and it generates nothing else.

Light by band Locked

Design principle: light must have a source you can lose. Ambient glow everywhere means darkness stops meaning anything.

BandLight
ThresholdReal sunlight. Shafts, doline groves, the sun crossing a cave wall over a day.
ShallowsBioluminescence. Living light: it moves, it can be farmed and eaten, and it goes out when frightened.
Mid ReachesEngineered light. The dead built lamps and some still work — you read by something's grave goods.
Under DeepsMineral, anomalous, wrong-coloured. Light with no obvious source.
RootNothing.

Darkvision: ordinary darkness in the Threshold and Shallows, where darkvision works normally and confers a real colonial advantage. But the Under Deeps are qualitatively dark — dark that behaves like a substance rather than an absence, which darkvision does not penetrate. This re-levels the party at depth without nerfing anyone's ancestry choice, it's a session-zero fact rather than a gotcha, and by that level everyone has magical answers.

Air and the standing drafts Locked

Before the Weeping most of the deep was unbreathable — sealed caves accumulate carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, methane and radon. Miners have real vocabulary for this worth stealing wholesale: blackdamp, firedamp, stinkdamp, whitedamp. Then twenty chimneys were punched through the crust, and the planet started breathing.

With a cold deep the circulation is more interesting than a simple chimney. The Mid Reaches are the engine — the hot band heats air, which rises and vents upward. Cold surface air is drawn down other Undertears to replace it. Root air is dense, pools, and doesn't circulate at all, which is why the deepest band stays stagnant and lethal even though everything above now ventilates.

  • Some Undertears breathe in, some breathe out. An exhaling Tear vents warm, wet, foul air — permanent fog over the Lip, nothing growing, the smell of the deep over the boomtown. An inhaling one pulls debris, birds and careless people down.
  • Direction flips seasonally. A colony venting its forges up an exhaling shaft is in trouble the month it reverses.
  • A breathable frontier is advancing. A moving boundary between air you can breathe and air you can't, pushing deeper monthly. A depth gate that isn't arbitrary.
  • Weather underground. Pressure fronts, fog banks, storms in the larger voids.
  • The worst squalls cluster at the edge of the Stillness — moving air meeting motionless air shears violently. Wind data is intelligence data: players can triangulate insectoid borders before ever meeting one.

Time underground Locked

Michel Siffre spent months alone in caves in the 1960s and 70s and lost roughly twenty-five days of count. Without light cues, circadian rhythm free-runs long.

  • Expedition drift. Parties return from long delves having lost days and arguing about the date. Colony bells keep surface time at the Shelf; below the Verge, nobody knows.
  • No generational drift — there hasn't been time. No deep-born colonists exist yet.
  • The fungal calendar is native, not colonial. Certain fungi bloom on a cycle and pulse light; the peoples who actually live down there time their lives by when the light comes on. Not a colonist custom — a discovery players earn by learning to read it.

Why hasn't a level 18 person fixed this? Open

Every setting with a ticking doom owes this answer. Combinable:

  • They're competing with each other. Every surface power has a champion, and all of them are pointed at each other rather than at the dark.
  • Scale defeats power. A level 20 archmage is one person in a labyrinth the size of a planet. Power doesn't solve search.
  • They went down and didn't come back. The first wave of legendary heroes descended in Year One. Their bodies are set-dressing and their gear is treasure — loot with names.
  • The threat isn't legible. You can't send a hero at a rumour.
Proposed

Use all four, weighted to the first. The strongest version of this campaign is one where the surface's inability to cooperate is the real antagonist, and the insectoids are merely the deadline.

04The Undertears

Anatomy — a reusable skeleton Proposed

Give every Tear the same five parts so you can improvise new ones instantly, then vary one thing each.

  1. The Lip — the surface rim. Cracked farmland, collapsed villages, now a boomtown. Whoever holds it taxes everything.
  2. The Throat — the vertical shaft. The engineering problem that defines the colony: winch-towers, stairs cut into rock, cable-cars, levitation platforms, a bridge spiralling down.
  3. The Shelf — where the shaft meets the first horizontal space. The actual town, built on scaffold.
  4. The Verge — the edge of surveyed territory. Where the map ends and the campaign starts.
  5. The Mouths — the tunnels onward. Usually several. Usually contested.

The consequence nobody thought about Proposed

You tore twenty holes kilometres deep into a planet. The rivers found them.

Where a Tear opened near a watershed, the river now pours into the ground. Downstream: dead farmland, abandoned cities, refugees, war over what water remains. Underground: permanent enormous waterfalls, new flooded regions, deep ecosystems drowned or born.

  • It makes descent non-optional. Nobody's going down for treasure alone — the world above is failing. That moralises your gold rush.
  • It gives you your best single image: a river falling into the earth, forever, lit by a shaft of daylight. That's your cover art and your first sketch.
  • Free political conflict: upstream and downstream nations now hate each other.
  • A second, ecological clock running alongside the invasion.

The dams Locked

The surface has responded by damming what it can. Not saved — but not collapsing either. Tone: managed crisis.

  • Uneven by design. Only the wealthiest or most magically capable nations managed a dam in two months, and even those are half-built. Some will fail.
  • This is your political map. Nations that were saved and nations that were ruined, and everyone knows which is which.
  • Dams consumed the money that could have funded descent. A nation with a dam came late to the Undermarch.
  • A dam is a chokepoint someone can sabotage. Breaking one is an act of war — or an insectoid acceleration of the clock.
  • Open: how were they built so fast? Earth-moving ritual magic is the plausible answer, and it implies a class of caster whose price just went up enormously.

Prep discipline for the twenty Proposed

You do not build twenty Undertears. You build:

  • One in full — the home base. NPCs, factions, economy, maps.
  • Two in sketch — a paragraph, a defining feature, one hook.
  • Seventeen as one-line rumours — a name and a single strange fact. "The Ninth is frozen solid and something is singing in it." That's the whole entry. Nine words doing 90% of the work.

05The antagonists

Resolved — civilization, non-negotiable

The single most consequential decision in the project. Hive-mind: one will, no negotiation, pure external threat. Clean, scary, and thin — you get a good final battle and nothing else. Players never meet a person, only a weather system. Civilization with castes: individuals, dissent, art, grief, internal politics, factions who disagree about the invasion. Everything gets better.

My recommendation: civilization, strongly. Keep the alien physiology — castes, chemical language, radial architecture, incomprehensible aesthetics — but give them politics. The uncanny should come from how they think, not whether they think.

Proposed

Homebrew the species on Formian caste architecture with Thriae magical culture layered on top. Statblocks to reskin at every level, plus a society where spellcasting is an institution rather than a talent — which is what gives "we spent a generation of mages on the ritual" its weight.

Proposed — the fungus-farming hook

Real ants and termites farm fungus. If yours do too, the deep mushroom forests are their agriculture — some cultivated, some abandoned centuries ago and gone feral. Your ecology and your antagonist become the same fact, and a party that learns to read fungal patterns can tell how long ago the insectoids were somewhere. A tracking skill, a dating method and a source of dread at once.

Reference worth mining: Golarion's Darklands run in three descending layers, and the deepest holds the Vaults of Orv — enormous engineered caverns, some with artificial suns, built by a vanished civilization. Your sunlit groves and forgotten cities, serial numbers already filed off.

The motive problem Proposed

"They want the surface" isn't a motive, it's a direction. Why the surface, and why now? The strongest answer makes them sympathetic without making them safe: their world is dying and the surface is the only exit.

CauseFlavour
The Root is coolingTheir whole ecology depends on deep heat, and it's going out.
Something eats the deepHorror. Not invaders — refugees, running from a thing you meet at level 18.
Their own ritual poisoned homeThe Tears cost them their world. Tragic and self-inflicted.
The floodwaters are drowning themDirect causal link to the rivers. Elegant.
Resolved — session 3

Answer: heat loss, via the fourth option's logic redirected at the Root entity rather than the floodwaters. The surface accidentally drowns the deep; the deep deliberately invades the surface; both think they're the wronged party. That's a campaign about a misunderstanding neither side can afford to have — and it gives your players something to do other than kill.

The Stillness Locked

A powerful enchantment holds the insectoid heartland untouched by the Weeping's side effects. Inside it there is no wind, no current, no draft, no intruding surface air or water — while the entire rest of the Undermarch howls. Their ecology is unfazed. Their homeland is not drowning and not yet freezing. It is fading, and it cannot be renewed.

Why it can't be renewed

The Stillness runs on deep heat — it always did, because that was the only abundant energy source down there. The entity at the Root is draining that heat. One cause, two symptoms: the homeland freezes and its only protection dissolves, faster than it can be re-gathered.

This is the entire motive. Their real enemy is beneath them, not above. The invasion is what a competent civilization does when it is losing a war it cannot win against something it may not be able to perceive. When the players understand this, "help us fight the thing at the Root" becomes a genuinely available move.

Their actual flaw

They were right about physics and wrong about people. They modelled the air, the water and the thermal load correctly. What they did not model was how fast the surface would come down the holes — they expected fearful primitives to shun the wounds in the earth for a generation. Instead: twenty colonies in two months.

Contempt for other minds, not incompetence at engineering. That flaw generates diplomacy, defectors, and an internal faction arguing the surface has been catastrophically misjudged.

  • Crossing the boundary is a sensory event. The roar simply stops. Nothing moves. One of the strangest moments available to you.
  • The boundary is receding, and it can be measured. Abandoned outposts sit just outside the current line; old markers stand in howling wind they were built to exclude. A party that maps the line twice, months apart, has quantified the enemy's timeline better than any prophecy.
  • Shear turbulence at the edge makes wind data into intelligence data.

Open: one enchantment or many? Who maintains it? Is there a caste whose entire existence is tending it — and what happens to them when it dies?

Structural questions Open

  • What do they call themselves? What do surface people call them — probably something crude ("chitters," "the crawl")?
  • How do they communicate — chemical, vibrational, sung? Can it be learned? If yes, that's a skill investment that pays off in a campaign-defining scene.
  • Do they have a religion? What do they believe the surface is — a ceiling, a heaven, a lie?
  • Caste structure: how many, how rigid, any mobility? Is there a caste that wasn't built for war and doesn't want one?
  • Is there a defector? There should be a defector. Your single most valuable NPC.
  • What did the ritual cost them, specifically and namably?

06The dead civilizations

Your instinct — sun-averse peoples migrating down while the ways were open — is good and load-bearing. But it needs a why did it close, and that's one of your biggest lore levers.

The era stack Proposed

Build the dead as stratigraphy: distinct eras, physically layered, deepest = oldest. Then the megadungeon teaches history through architecture, and a player who learns to read building styles can date a ruin at a glance. That's a real skill your players will develop and enjoy.

EraDepthCharacter
The BuildersRoot / Under DeepsPre-human, non-humanoid. Scale wrong, doorways wrong, no stairs. Nobody knows what they were.
The SunfledUnder Deeps / MidYour migration. Recognisably civilised — plazas, temples, drainage — which makes them the most haunting ruins, because they look like home.
The Sealing EraMid / ShallowsWhatever closed the ways. Fortifications, mass graves, enormous doors barred from above. Your central historical mystery.
The AftermathShallowsIsolated survivors. Small, degenerate or transformed, some still alive and very surprised to see you.
Blocker — why did the ways close?

A war? A plague? A deliberate quarantine by surface powers? Did the insectoids seal it? Did something get out once and it was never allowed to happen again? This determines whether the Undertears are a tragedy, an escape, or a repetition — probably your most important lore decision after the agency question.

The living deep Open

You said no classic-underground-trope monoculture — good. Seeds worth developing, most of them real:

  • Chemosynthetic zones. Movile Cave in Romania has been sealed 5.5 million years and runs on sulfur, not sunlight. Alien food web, everything pale. Free weirdness, zero handwaving.
  • Doline forests. Hang Son Doong has collapsed ceilings admitting sunlight, and there is jungle inside the cave. Your sunlit groves are geologically legitimate.
  • Fungal succession — not one mushroom forest but several, ecologically distinct: rot-fed, corpse-fed, one that's farmed and one that farms you.
  • Thermal zones — hot springs, mineral terraces, civilizations built around heat like a hearth.
  • The drowned — flooded regions still filling. A region actively becoming a sea during the campaign.
  • Aphotic megafauna — what's the apex predator, what do locals think it is, and are they wrong?

07The clock

Critique — your two goals are fighting

Megadungeon play is exploratory: non-linear, slow, digressive, curiosity-driven. A one-year invasion clock is urgent: focused, directed, punishing of digression. Run them naively together and either the players ignore the clock (and the invasion feels like a GM ambush) or they chase it and never explore (wasting the megadungeon you built).

The resolution

Make exploration the method of fighting the clock. The counter-invasion isn't found on the surface — it's down there: in the Weftway network they must map, in Sealing Era records of how the ways were closed before, in dead civilizations' weapons, in a defector's testimony, in the politics of a deep city nobody knew was inhabited. Every delve is both treasure hunt and a move against the clock. Then digression isn't failure — it's a different route.

Clock design Proposed

An eight-segment Front clock, advancing on triggers, not the calendar — calendar-doom punishes careful play and rewards nothing. Triggers: ignoring a known Weftway node; a surface faction doing something shortsighted (this should be most of them); a failed major objective; plus slow fixed advancement at downtime milestones so it never fully stalls.

Make it legible. One escalating sign per segment:

  1. Tremors at the Verge.
  2. Livestock and pack animals refuse the Mouths.
  3. Insectoid scouts appear two depth bands shallower than they should.
  4. An outlying survey camp goes silent.
  5. A Weftway node opens inside surveyed territory.
  6. A minor Undertear colony falls. Refugees arrive at yours.
  7. The Throat of a major Undertear is contested.
  8. Invasion.
Open — this is where level range stops being deferrable

One in-world year is compressed for 1→20. Either (a) the campaign covers the year and ends around 12–14 with the invasion as climax; (b) players successfully stall the clock and it continues into a longer war, reaching 20; or (c) the year is arc one of three. The depth bands in §03 depend on this answer.

08PF2e mechanical layer

Critique — the attrition problem

This will bite you, and most homebrew megadungeon GMs don't see it coming. Classic megadungeon play runs on attrition — torches burn, rations dwindle, spells don't come back. PF2e is built to defeat exactly that. Focus Points return in 10 minutes. Treat Wounds restores the party out of combat for free. Its encounter math assumes you start near full.

Do nothing and your vast dangerous labyrinth becomes a series of unrelated corridor fights with a picnic between each one.

Fixes, in the order I'd recommend them:

  1. Make pressure environmental, not resource-based. Heat, air quality, darkness, unstable terrain, corruption and afflictions that persist past rests. Afflictions do survive downtime — that's your attrition mechanism, already in the rules.
  2. Adapt Hexploration into Delve-ploration. Kingmaker's subsystem maps almost directly: activities per travel period, discovery rolls, supply expenditure. Reskin the hex as a "reach."
  3. Supply as hard currency, not bookkeeping. Don't track rations. Track Supply as an abstract pool; spend it to rest safely. Out of Supply means no Treat Wounds, no Refocus, no full rest. Now the depth you can reach is a function of what you carried — a real decision.
  4. Use Victory Point / Influence subsystems for colony and faction politics. They're in GM Core, they're good, and this campaign is 40% politics whether you plan it or not.
  5. Do not nerf Treat Wounds by fiat. Players hate it and it just makes combat swingier. Constrain where they can rest, not whether healing works.

Light and darkvision Proposed

Most PF2e ancestries have low-light vision; true darkvision is rarer. In a campaign set entirely underground that's not a footnote — it's one of your strongest atmospheric tools and a live balance question.

  • Ancestry becomes a setting statement. Dwarves, orcs and similar have a colonial advantage. That has political consequences: who gets hired as guides, who runs the deep trade, who resents whom.
  • Light attracts things. A party with lanterns is a moving beacon in a world where everything else navigates without light. Tie encounter frequency to light discipline and your players will make interesting choices unprompted.
  • Open: is light scarce? If yes, magical light becomes premier treasure and the whole deep economy runs on it.

Statblock sourcing Proposed

Don't build from scratch. Reskin and re-level: Formians (existing hive-society lineage, closest fit for castes), Ankhravs (mid-level bruisers), Mukradi and assorted vermin from Monster Core for the bestiary spine. Build three or four signature insectoids yourself at different levels and reskin everything else — players remember silhouettes, not stat blocks.

09The map program

Do not start with the world map

The most common worldbuilding mistake and the one that wastes the most effort. The world map is the least useful thing you can draw right now, because you don't yet know what needs to be on it.

Draw these, in this order

#MapWhy now
01Cross-section of the FallwaterYour setting's defining property is verticality, and you can't design vertically until you've drawn vertically. Forces you to answer the physical questions you're deferring: how deep, how wide, how does anyone descend, where does light stop, where does the river fall. Best table prop you'll ever make.
02The colony at the ShelfVertical, built on scaffold and rope, clinging to a shaft wall. Draw it isometric or sectional, not top-down. Players spend every non-delving hour here.
03The local reach — as a node mapCircles for locations, lines for routes, annotations for hazards and travel time. Precise cave cartography is a trap: it takes forever and nobody navigates a real cave map at the table, they navigate a route.
04One dungeon site, in fullA single Sunfled ruin or Sealing fortification. Gridded, ready to run. Just one — prove the pipeline before scaling it.
05The world schematic. Last.Continents in rough mass, twenty Tears marked, watersheds indicated, surface powers blocked in. Resist detail. Nobody will ever be there.

Analog kit

  • Isometric grid paper — the unlock for cross-sections and scaffold-towns. Square grid fights you on verticality.
  • Dot grid for dungeon interiors; far less visually noisy than square grid.
  • Fineliners in three weights (005 / 01 / 03) — hairlines for hatching, medium for walls, heavy for the frame. Consistent line weight is what makes hand-drawn maps look professional.
  • Tracing / layout paper — draw the rock once, overlay different states (flooded, insectoid-held, ruined). Enormously useful for a campaign where places change.
  • Hachure marks — the standard cartographic convention for pit edges and drop-offs. Learn these; they make vertical maps instantly readable.

11The Fallwater — home Undertear

Locked The Undertear pictured in Fig. 1. Tone: grim survival.

Identity

Two names, same device as the event itself. Concord's Reach on every charter document and toll receipt. The Fallwater to everyone who actually lives there.

The watershed answers to no single crown — a half-dozen minor lords and a bankrupt duchy share it, none capable of a full dam alone. No strong state means no single authority controlled the Undertear when it opened, which is what makes the triangle possible. Layout follows the anatomy template in §04: the Lip is scaffolding and Concord's toll tower, the Throat is the shaft with the river falling through, the Shelf is the settlement proper, the Verge is the edge of the surveyed, the Mouths lead into the unknown.

12Early dungeon sites near the Fallwater

Proposed Three sites, all reachable from the Fallwater without a long expedition, spanning levels 1–5 and three different modes of play: vertical/environmental, social/tragic, hazard/negotiation. None require the antagonists to be resolved. Small, deliberate seeds of the deeper mystery sit in each — nothing that commits you to an answer yet.

The Drowned Chapel Levels 1–2

Location. Wedged into the Throat wall of the Fallwater itself, roughly 40 metres below the Lip. Visible from the winch-tower on a still day, swaying slightly in the draft.

Premise. The village chapel, caught on a ledge the Throat carved as it tore open. It didn't fall — it's held. The congregation was mid-service. Most didn't get out.

Shape of the site. Not a corridor crawl — a climb through a tilted building. Load-bearing pews, a bell that still hangs and will ring if disturbed (a Throat Squall gust could set it off unprompted). Bodies remain; some recoverable for the Vigil's rites, some showing signs looters have already been through.

The twist

A survivor is still alive, sealed into the sacristy for two months, rationing communion wine and altar bread. How the party handles this — rescue, loot around them, report to a faction — is a strong character-defining choice, and exactly the kind of hard, human-scale decision the grim-survival tone should run on.

Faction pull. The Vigil wants the bodies and relics and will pay or grant standing for retrieval. Concord may want the lead roofing and the bell salvaged. A contained, low-stakes introduction to the triangle's competing claims.

The Hushed of the Undersong Levels 3–4

Location. A day or two past the Verge, in a side-passage newly ventilated by the Weeping — sealed with bad air until the Undertears started the world breathing.

Premise. A pocket of survivors called the Hushed — not fresh refugees, but descendants of something older, cut off since the Sealing Era, who believed they were the last people in the world. Pale, sensitive-eyed, sustained for generations on cultivated fungus and mineral light.

The cruelty of the timing

The new air currents are killing their food supply — their fungus depended on the old, sealed air chemistry. The same event that let the party find them is quietly destroying them.

Shape of the site. A small settlement, not a combat gauntlet — narrow galleries, fungus terraces going brown, defensive traps built generations ago against something the Hushed call only "the old fear." An elder willing to trade lore for aid, if aid can even be offered.

Why it matters. Rich roleplay and moral weight rather than loot. A live thread toward the parked "why did the ways close" question — the Hushed may hold oral history nobody living has heard. "The old fear" is a controlled, deniable hint you can develop later without committing to what it means yet.

Faction pull. The Vigil would be desperate for contact — proof of doctrine, or a crisis of faith. Concord would want to catalogue or exploit them. The Commons would see kindred desperate people worth aiding. One site, three different reasons to care.

The Cinderspore Reach Levels 2–5, scalable

Location. Near the Verge, along an exhaling Mouth thick with bioluminescence.

Premise. A vast, richly productive bioluminescent fungal grove — the first significant renewable food and medicine source found near the Fallwater. Exactly what "Winter Is Two Months Away" needs, and all three factions want it: Concord to toll it, the Commons to survive on it, the Vigil to read it as an omen — light where there should be none.

Shape of the site. Environmental-hazard-forward rather than monster-forward. Standing drafts move spore clouds through the galleries. A Blackdamp Pocket sits in the grove's lowest hollow. Unstable footing on fungal terraces. Beneath the growth, old cultivation terraces from someone much older — and half-buried in one terrace, a tool that matches no known culture.

A second First Sign

Delivered as a detail in a location rather than a single object — for a table that already found the first one at the toll post and wants confirmation rather than a fresh mystery.

Faction pull. A natural site to be hired as guard, scout or negotiator by any of the three factions — or to broker a three-way deal between them. Where the Victory Point / Influence subsystem would actually earn its keep, if you want mechanics under the negotiation.

The Standing Draft

Every Throat and most major tunnels carry a permanent wind. Ambient, not a hazard — it applies constantly and shapes how players plan.

Throat Squall Hazard 5

Complex · Environmental

Stealth DC 20 (expert) — a pressure drop in the ears and a rising howl precede it by a few seconds

Description The air column surges as thermal fronts collide kilometres below, or as the draft scrapes past the edge of something that will not let it in.

Disable Survival DC 22 (read the signs and brace the party) or Athletics DC 24 (fix and secure a line before it hits)

AC 22; Fort +14, Ref +8

Immunities object immunities; can't be attacked or damaged

Squall [reaction] Trigger A creature enters the Throat, or the squall begins. Effect The hazard rolls initiative.

Routine (2 actions) Both actions each round for 3 rounds, then it subsides.

Surge — each creature in the Throat attempts a DC 22 Fortitude save.

  • Success Pushed 5 feet along the draft.
  • Failure 2d6 bludgeoning and pushed 15 feet along the draft.
  • Critical failure 4d6 bludgeoning, pushed 30 feet, and DC 22 Reflex or lose its handhold.

Torn debris — rope ends, cargo, scaffold boards, loose stone. One random creature in the Throat takes 3d6 bludgeoning, DC 22 Reflex basic save.

Reset Squalls unpredictably, roughly once per hour. Near the edge of the Stillness, roughly once per ten minutes.

Design note. In a horizontal tunnel this is a nasty but survivable buffeting. In a vertical Throat the push is a fall. Same hazard, radically different threat depending on geography — which teaches players to respect terrain rather than numbers. Scale to other bands by adjusting DCs and dice; the shape holds.

Blackdamp Pocket Hazard 2

Environmental

Stealth DC 18 (trained) — flames gutter and burn low; a carried bird sickens first

Description A hollow where oxygen-depleted air has settled and stayed. Odourless, invisible, heavier than good air, so it pools in dips, sumps and dead-end chambers.

Disable Survival DC 18 (recognise the signs and withdraw) or Crafting DC 20 (rig a draft-line to ventilate)

Suffocation [reaction] Trigger A creature enters the pocket or begins its turn there. Effect DC 18 Fortitude save.

  • Success No effect, but the creature knows something is wrong.
  • Failure Fatigued, and can't recover until it spends 10 minutes in clean air.
  • Critical failure As failure, and the creature falls unconscious and begins suffocating.

Reset Refills within an hour unless the chamber is permanently ventilated.

Design note. Your quiet attrition hazard — it doesn't deal damage, it spends the party. Fatigued characters make worse decisions three rooms later. Makes air a planning problem rather than a damage problem.

  • Signature insectoid stat blocks — three or four across the level range, everything else reskinned
