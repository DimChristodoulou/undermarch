Common Knowledge — what characters know or can learn

The World

Two months ago, the Weeping tore roughly twenty vast shafts open across the world, connecting the surface to a vast underground labyrinth people call the Undermarch — or, among those who've lived there long enough to love it, the Undersky. Rivers near several of these openings now drain into the earth; surface nations have built dams where they could, though not every region managed it in time. The openings sit far apart on a planet-scale map, and travel between them — over the surface or through the deep — is a genuinely difficult undertaking.

The Surface World

Six continents divide the world's landmass: Kethal, Naidrora, Aubannon, Obun, Emmux, and Jomoth. Kethal is the largest at roughly fifteen million square kilometres, and the Undertear Compact was founded there — at Xynnar, in Velmack. Obun is the smallest, at roughly five million. Like the calendar, the names are old survey terms carried forward out of administrative habit; people say them daily and none of them know what they originally meant.

Kethal holds five nations: Velmack, whose capital is Xynnar; Brannock; the Tenemrea Kingdom; Surlund; and Cludonea. Most are multiancestral. No nation on Kethal belongs to a single ancestry as a matter of course. The five hold most of Kethal's settled land but not all of its territory — beyond them lie frontier, minor lordships, and stretches nobody has surveyed since the survey terms were coined.

Brannock is the exception. It is an elven nation, and it holds deep forest and open desert both, which is why woodland, ancient, and desert lines are all native to it rather than immigrant. An elf of any of the three can be from Brannock without further explanation. Its borders are as fixed and as formally recognised as any on the continent; what sets it apart is contact, not legitimacy. The other four nations deal with Brannock through a single licensed border market and know correspondingly little about the interior.

Obun lies far north, and it is cold. Its growing season is short, its soil is poor, and it has never fed itself. Corrach-Nom holds roughly four fifths of it — a dwarven and gnomish nation occupying the highland interior, and the most technically accomplished polity in the world. Nine smaller nations hold the coasts. They arrived by sea some three and a half thousand years ago, found the interior already held, and settled the harbours instead; they are trading states rather than agricultural ones, and their business is moving overseas grain inland and Corrach-Nom metalwork outward.

Nothing about this arrangement is charity in either direction. Corrach-Nom's smiths and engineers supply the continent — and much of the world — with metal, tools, weapons and armour, and its military advantage over its neighbours is decisive in any engagement not decided by spellcasters. It also cannot eat any of that. The coastal nations hold the harbours, the ships, and the grain contracts, and a continent that cannot feed itself does not conquer its own suppliers. There has been no war on Obun in living memory. That is a structural fact rather than a sentimental one, and there are factions among the nine who regard the Diarchy's restraint as patience rather than good will.

Corrach-Nom grows what it can underground — fungal farms and cavern cultivation, inherited technique carried out at scale. It is enough to survive a bad year and nowhere near enough to live on. The bulk of the food comes in by sea, and a great deal of it originates in the Tenemrea Kingdom, whose harvests therefore matter on a continent most Tenemreans will never see.

Corrach-Nom's own account of itself begins in the dark. Its oldest tales say the two peoples came up out of the deep into an empty world and found it theirs to walk into, and that a dwarven king and a gnomish queen married to bind mining, smithing and smelting to tinkering and invention. Nobody treats the first part as literal history. It is the sort of thing every nation says about its founding, and Corrach-Nom says it at feasts without believing it.

For the past thousand years the state has been the Corrach-Nom Diarchy, and it is not a monarchy in any ordinary sense. Two crowns rule together, always one dwarf and one gnome, and the pairing alternates each reign: a dwarven king with a gnomish queen, then a gnomish king with a dwarven queen. The present pair is a gnome king and a dwarf queen. Both are elected — separately — by a council of a hundred, fifty dwarves and fifty gnomes, and wed at coronation; the marriage is a marriage of state, and because the crowns are elective it needs no heirs. Council seats are allocated fifty-fifty by law and filled from the guilds — miners, craftsmen, tinkers, and every other trade that matters — with each guild electing its dwarven and gnomish delegates separately. The guilds themselves are not divided by race. Scientists, engineers and soldiers reach office the same way everyone else does, through a guild.

A tied vote is broken by the outgoing monarch of the opposite race, which means every reign has a hand in choosing its successor's counterpart. Monarchs serve for life, with one exception: the council may judge a royal pair unworthy and remove them. It judges the pair. Neither crown can be removed alone, and neither can survive the other's disgrace — which is the practical reason the two of them hold together.

Three Undertears opened inside Obun's territories. Corrach-Nom keeps its best engineers and scholars at home to work them, and sends smaller delegations abroad.

The world is volcanically violent and always has been. Ash seasons are a fixed part of the agricultural calendar everywhere, and every child learns which mountains to watch and when. Nobody connects this to anything.

The Sky

Two moons cross the sky. Aumbris is the larger and paler of them, and it is the moon the world sets its calendar by: a full cycle runs thirty days, and a month means one turn of Aumbris everywhere anyone keeps records. The month names are old survey terms like the rest of the almanac and mean nothing to the people who say them; the month length is the moon, and nobody finds that a strange arrangement. Velkarr is smaller, closer, faster, and red. Charters and almanacs use both names. Common speech does not — Aumbris is the Warden, and Velkarr is the Ember.

Velkarr runs twice for every one turn of Aumbris, so the two line up once a month, on the same date, forever. That much is in every almanac. Velkarr itself is not predictable at all. Aumbris shows the same face a grandfather saw; Velkarr's dark patches drift across a season and are sometimes repainted outright in the space of a few days, after which every sky-chart of it is wrong. Rarely, at crescent, a faint spike stands off its edge for a night or two. Nobody has explained any of this and nobody has instruments fine enough to try.

The monthly alignment puts both pulls on the same side of the world and produces the highest tides of the month, on a fixed and published date. On most coasts this is an inconvenience. On Surlund's shoal channels it is the difference between a passage and a wreck, and it is part of what the pilot guilds are actually selling. Velkarr's path is tilted a few degrees off Aumbris's, so it crosses in front of it only once every few years rather than every month; where that is visible, it is an occasion.

Neither moon reaches below the Threshold. Where a doline opens to the sky — the Skywell and its like — moonlight falls in the same shifting column the sun does, weaker, and on a cycle nobody underground has yet bothered to write down.

The Undermarch

The deep is not one connected cave system. It's a series of isolated regions separated by gaps nothing ordinary can cross — flooded sumps, magma sheets, solid rock — each reachable independently from the surface but not easily from one another.

Danger and difficulty increase with depth, loosely recognized in bands: the Threshold near the surface, the Shallows beneath it, the Mid Reaches further still. Few living explorers have gone deeper than the Mid Reaches and returned to describe it.

Every Undertear shares the same rough anatomy: a surface Lip, where the settlement and its winch-towers sit; a long vertical Throat dropping clean and largely uninterrupted toward the Root; Mouths, where the Throat's fall happens to intersect a pre-existing cave system, some large enough to hold a small outpost but none of them a second colony; and a Verge marking the edge of whatever's been surveyed so far, wherever that happens to stop. They are enormous. A Throat is on the order of forty kilometres across — wide enough that the far wall is a smudge on the horizon in clear air, and wide enough to hold its own weather. There is exactly one settlement per Undertear, at the Lip. Anything deeper is at most a handful of people in a Mouth, not a town. Nobody climbs an Undertear: descent and ascent are by engineered lift from the winch-towers at the Lip, and there has never been a rope-and-piton route down a Throat. Signalling between the two ends of that lift is done with paired bells.

The top few hundred metres of any Throat wall cut cleanly through the Floor (see below), which is why the upper shaft reads as banded rather than uniform stone — grey ash courses, a horizon of flattened timber, and occasionally worked masonry standing in cross-section. Surveyors use the banding as a depth reference. Nobody has explained it.

Light changes character with depth — real sun near the surface, living bioluminescence in the Shallows, the ruined lamps of dead civilizations deeper still, and eventually a darkness that ordinary means of seeing in the dark simply fail against. Every Throat carries a permanent wind; some Undertears exhale warm air upward, others inhale it down. Miners have old words for bad air — blackdamp, firedamp, stinkdamp, whitedamp — and know narrow, unventilated pockets are best avoided. Expeditions that spend long enough below lose track of the day without sunlight to mark it.

The Paired Bells

Three bells hang in a rack on one side of the winch at the bottom of its run, and three more hang in the winch-tower at the Lip. Each bell below was cast in the same pour as its partner above and carries the same rune cut into its lip; strike one and the other sounds, with however much rock between them. The runes read up, down and help, and that is the entire vocabulary. A crew at the bottom rings up to be hauled, rings down to have the cage sent back empty, and rings the third when something has gone wrong badly enough that the winch-hands should not wait to be told what. The pairing runs both ways, so the Lip answers on the same three: down from above means the cage is already coming, and help from above means come back now, no argument.

The enchantment is old and unremarkable. Paired bells have hung in mineheads, keeps and harbour towers for centuries and nothing about making them is new — which is the only reason a two-month-old colony has a set at all. They are expensive rather than rare. The Fallwater's were bought, not invented, and the Compact owns them exactly as it owns the towers they hang in, which means the Compact decides who is permitted to ring for help.

What the bells cannot do shapes everything below them. A bell rung is a bell heard and nothing more: nobody at the Lip can tell who pulled the rope, or why, or whether anyone is still standing by the time the cage arrives. Nothing deeper than the winch's bottom landing is paired to anything, so an expedition that leaves the landing and walks into a Mouth has walked away from the only line it had. And anyone who reaches the rack can ring them, which is a fact the winch-hands think about more than they say.

The Era Stack

The deep is layered, and the layers are historical. Four eras are recognized, deepest to shallowest: the Builders, whose architecture is pre-human and reads as alien to everyone who has seen it; the Sunfled, sun-averse migrants who were recognisably civilized and recognisably came from above; the Sealing Era, whoever closed the ways between surface and deep, known by fortifications and mass graves; and the Aftermath, isolated survivors living on in pockets afterward. Because the stack is physically stratified, architecture teaches history — a surveyor who can date a wall can date the level it stands on.

How anyone left the deep after the ways were sealed is not accounted for. The stack says the Aftermath survived; it does not say how any of them got out, and nothing found so far explains it. See Open Questions.

Factions

Three organizations operate across the Undermarch. All three are global bodies, present in some form at most Undertear settlements rather than confined to any single site, and where they appear they appear because they have their own reasons to be there — not because no local authority exists.

The Sounding Line — scholars, engineers, spelunkers, and alchemists organized to survey the Undermarch: what caused the Weeping, whether it could happen again, and what the Undertears actually connect to below. It draws its people from every nation on the planet, and at any given Undertear the host nation's scholars outnumber the rest — the Line at the Fallwater is mostly Velmack's, the Line in Obun is mostly Corrach-Nom's. This is not a compromise of its purpose. The Line pursues knowledge sincerely, and it is understood by everyone that governments read what their own citizens send home. Chronically understaffed against the scale of the task, the Line rarely fields its own expeditions past a Verge — it sponsors them instead, buying survey data, specimens, and reports from anyone willing to sell. An internal divide runs through it: researchers who favor publishing findings openly, against a more cautious circle that has already withheld at least one discovery it judged too dangerous to release. Two Line products have become universal below ground: sounding lanterns, which burn without fuel and lean toward the nearest way to the surface, and Line cable, which does not part under load. Almost every expedition carries both, whoever it works for.

The lanterns are not the Line's invention. Corrach-Nom developed them and handed over the schematics, and the price was not money — the Diarchy has little use for foreign coin. It took guaranteed grain tonnage, and a permanent Diarchy seat in Line survey operations at every Undertear on the planet. At the time it was widely read as generosity, since a lamp that points home saves lives and withholding it would have been monstrous. The cable is the Line's own work, and the Line has never shared it.

The Undertear Compact — a multinational agreement placing soldiers from rival nations under joint command at Undertear frontiers, formed to stop any single power from unilaterally seizing the new openings. It was founded at Xynnar, in Velmack, which remains a live grievance: every other Kethali nation signed away unilateral control of its own openings in a building in Velmack's capital, and Velmack governs the Fallwater in fact all the same. Nations ruined by the Weeping's floods pushed hardest for it; nations that finished their dams in time joined more reluctantly. In practice the Compact is an uneasy coalition rather than a unified army — national contingents distrust one another, and home governments issue contradictory orders behind its back. Its public position is that the Undertears were dug — deliberately, by something — and that the surface should prepare for what climbs out. Most member nations privately regard this as alarmist. Its quieter second purpose is commercial: nobody gets rich off an Undertear without the Compact's approval.

The Vigil of the Weeping — an old, fringe religious order with scattered chapter-houses that predate the Weeping, now reading the event as confirmation of doctrine it already held. Declared heretical by the mainstream Church, the Vigil provides medicine and burial rites wherever it holds ground, and its scholars catalogue deep anomalies more thoroughly than anyone else — understanding few of them. Vigil texts described an opening of the deep before the Weeping happened, in terms specific enough that dismissing the match has become awkward, and the order has grown enormously in the two months since. Its doctrine holds that the Weeping was an announcement: the herald of a god rising from below, to be met rather than fought.

The Fallwater

The home Undertear. Compact records designate it only by number; everyone who actually lives there calls it the Fallwater, after the river pouring through its Throat. The watershed above it lies inside Velmack, and Velmack governs it in fact as well as in name: the crown at Xynnar issues the charters, sets the tolls, and holds the final say over who goes down.

Velmack could have dammed the river. It chose not to. Xynnar did the arithmetic and judged an open Undertear worth more than the farmland below it, and let the farmland drown. The ruined country downstream knows exactly whose decision did that, and says so.

All three factions maintain a presence, and none of them is an outsider filling a vacuum. The Sounding Line keeps a survey chapter studying the Throat and its anomalies, needing no better reason than wanting to know what happened and why. The Compact holds the Lip and controls descent — and because Velmack was one of its founding nations, that garrison answers to the Compact and to Velmack at the same time. The Vigil keeps a chapter-house providing medicine and burial rites, and is difficult for any crown to refuse now that the Weeping appears to have proved it right. Between them they cover security, commerce, and the ordinary contentment of the people who live there.

Phenomena & Hazards

The Vanishing Waterfall. At the Fallwater, water falling down the Throat visibly breaks apart into mist within the first few hundred metres and never reaches bottom — caught instead in the shaft's rising warm draft, reappearing as the permanent fog that hangs over the Lip. Full mechanism on the Gameplay page.

Throat Squalls. Sudden, violent gusts strike unpredictably in the Throat and any tunnel connected to its airflow. Colonists know narrow side-passages are, if anything, more dangerous than the open shaft, not less. Full mechanism and stat block on the Gameplay page.

History of the Current Surface World — common knowledge on Kethal

Kethal has no continuous history older than roughly three thousand seven hundred years, and every nation on it knows that. What came before is folklore — flood-and-fire stories, a drowned country, a world that ended. Nobody treats any of it as history, and nobody has connected it to the ash seasons they still live with.

Wind, Ash, and Water

Kethal's prevailing winds run west to east. Everything that happens agriculturally on the continent follows from that. Moist air comes off the western ocean, rises over the Red Peaks, and drops its rain on the western slope; it comes down the eastern slope dry. The same wind carries volcanic ash east off the arc.

The result is that Tenemrea and the eastern half of Velmack hold the richest soil on the continent and the least reliable rainfall. Tenemrea is not a rain-fed country. It is an irrigation state: snowmelt and captured rain from the Red Peaks' western face drains east through a handful of large gorge-fed rivers, and Tenemrea has spent a thousand years canalising them. Ash supplies the fertility, the mountains supply the water, and the crown supplies the canals. A heavy ash year does not merely dust the fields — it chokes the canals, and clearing them is a recurring national emergency.

The Cinder Belt is the dry, ash-loaded strip immediately east of the Red Peaks, before the rivers regain enough water to matter. Grazing, quarry and charcoal country. Thinly held, and militarily important out of all proportion to what lives there.

Further north there is no eastern sea close enough to compensate, and the same rain shadow produces Brannock's open desert, Dunthara. Brannock's forest lies west and north of the range, in the wet; Dunthara lies east of it. One nation, one people, two climates, one mountain wall between them.

Eastern Velmack works more simply: it lies downwind of the Asbour's eastern end, receives reworked ash washed down off the range, and has ordinary rainfall. Good farmland rather than spectacular — enough to feed Velmack, not enough to feed anyone else.

The Floor

Everywhere on Kethal there is a buried layer. Farmers call it the Floor: sterile grey ash and welded tuff, sometimes with a horizon of flattened or upright timber in it, sometimes with worked stone. It is not at a consistent depth and it is not a thin seam. It runs three hundred metres and more beneath the Tenemrean basins and the Fallwater valley, perhaps forty across the open plains, and effectively nothing on ridges and old highlands where erosion has stripped it back to bedrock.

Nobody reaches it by digging — no one sinks a three-hundred-metre shaft on speculation. It is reached where geology exposes it: river gorges, sea cliffs, quarry faces, landslide scars, and now the upper walls of every Undertear Throat. This means knowledge of the Floor is a matter of where you live. Surlund's uplifted coast exposes it in cross-section for hundreds of kilometres and Surlunders have looked at it their whole lives; the interior plains have never seen it at all.

There is no accepted explanation. Local names are descriptive rather than theoretical — the Floor, the grey course, the drowned wood, the Old Ground. Fishermen on the Surlund coast will tell you the sea took a country once. It is treated exactly the way Corrach-Nom treats its own founding tale: a thing people say, not a thing people believe. See the Burial Ages for what is actually true, which nobody on the surface knows.

The Two Ranges

The Red Peaks run roughly north to south down the northern half of the continent, dividing Cludonea in the west from Tenemrea in the east, with Brannock straddling their northern end. They are a young, active volcanic arc and they hold the great majority of Kethal's active cones. The largest of them is the Ebon Summit. These are the mountains children are taught to watch. The name is for the iron-stained ash, not the rock.

The Asbour Mountains run roughly east to west across the middle of the continent, separating the northern nations from Velmack and Surlund. They are old, folded and extinct — no cones, no ash, worn down far enough that their ore sits exposed. This is where Kethal mines. The range is a shared frontier rather than any one nation's possession, and mining camps in it answer to whoever reached them last.

The two ranges do not meet. The Varngate Gap is the lowland corridor between the southern end of the Red Peaks and the western end of the Asbour, and it is the only easy overland route between northern and southern Kethal. Everything else is a mountain crossing or a sea voyage. Velmack holds it, from the fortress town of Varngate, and charges for it. This is a substantial part of why Velmack is the largest nation on the continent.

The Coasts and the Strait

Kethal's eastern coast is shoal for hundreds of kilometres — ash-loaded rivers carry enormous sediment loads and build sandbar and delta faster than the sea clears them. Deep-hulled ships cannot approach most of it. The bay it forms off Tenemrea is the Cassiran Bay. Cassira, Tenemrea's great eastern port, sits at the one place where a scoured channel runs close inshore, and dredging it is a permanent and expensive obligation of the Tenemrean crown. The year Tenemrea cannot afford the dredging is the year the grain does not sail.

The Cassiran Strait separates Kethal's southeastern mainland from the head of Surlund's island chain, and it is the only short passage between the continent's eastern and western coasts. It takes its name from the Cassira grain run, which is the traffic that made it worth holding. Surlund holds both shores and levies a toll on every hull that passes. The alternative is to round the whole southern tail of Surlund in open ocean off a lee shore, which costs weeks and, in bad seasons, ships. Nobody is obliged to pay. Almost everybody does.

Kethali borders were not drawn by geography or by treaty first. They were claimed by occupation, disputed, fought over, and only then written down. Where a modern border follows a river or a ridge, it is because someone stopped there — not because anyone agreed the river was the line.

The Five Nations

Velmack. The largest nation on Kethal and the most balanced. A strong standing military — the oldest on the continent — moderate agriculture on its eastern side, and ports on both coasts, which no other Kethali nation has. It holds the Varngate Gap and taxes the overland traffic through it, governs the Fallwater outright, and hosted the founding of the Undertear Compact at Xynnar. Its reputation among the other four is competence without warmth: Velmack is the nation that did the arithmetic on its own farmland and let it drown.

The Tenemrea Kingdom. An agricultural and commercial powerhouse, producing roughly sixty percent of the continent's food supply on the richest soil in the world. It has invested heavily and deliberately in agricultural advancement — canals, drainage, seed selection, crop rotation as state policy. Tenemrean wine is a delicacy served in noble courts well beyond Kethal, and the Ombrian vineyards on the Red Peaks' eastern footslopes are the most valuable ground on the continent by area. Tenemrea has money, food, and very little military weight, and every neighbour knows the ratio.

Cludonea. A major maritime force, built on import and export rather than production. Its decisive advantage is elemental magic: Cludonean work on air and water is the most advanced anywhere, and a Cludonean merchant ship carries a trained wizard as a matter of routine, holding wind and current to schedule. This makes Cludonean shipping faster and more predictable than anyone else's, and it is the reason Tenemrea's grain reaches Obun on time. The Calvyr Halls train every one of those wizards and the state controls who graduates.

Brannock. The elven nation, holding forest west of the Red Peaks' northern arm and desert east of it. Its borders are formally established and were the first on the continent to be fixed. What separates Brannock from the other four is contact rather than status: foreign traffic enters through a single licensed border market and goes no further, so the other nations have treaties with Brannock, embassies at its frontier, and almost no knowledge of its interior. Rumour fills the gap, and Brannock has never troubled to correct any of it.

Surlund. The southern nation, holding the southeastern mainland and the island chain running out from it. One of the two First Risings and therefore the oldest continuously settled ground on Kethal — and the poorest of the five. Its wealth is entirely positional: it holds both shores of the Cassiran Strait and tolls the only short route between Kethal's coasts. It has no fleet worth the name and does not need one, because the channels are shoal, tidal and unmarked, and a foreign hull without a Surlunder aboard is a wreck waiting to happen. The pilot guilds are as powerful as the crown and considerably older. Surlund's national temperament is decayed seniority: oldest ground, least power, and a long memory for both.

Unknown Knowledge — hidden from the world, for the GM only

The Weeping was not a natural disaster. It was deliberately caused — an underground civilization of insectoid mages tore the Undertears open in a single, immensely costly ritual, seeking access to the surface. Nobody above ground knows they exist.

These insectoids are a genuine civilization, not a mindless hive — castes, internal politics, dissent, art. Whatever the surface eventually learns about them will be more complicated than "monster."

Their homeland deep below is protected by a powerful enchantment shielding it from hazards the rest of the deep now faces. That enchantment is not infinite. It is fading, cannot be renewed, and is the true reason the insectoids need the surface — not conquest for its own sake, but a dying homeland with nowhere else to go.

The insectoids knew the period. They have come through Burial Ages before and were prepared for this one; the draw itself was expected and planned around. What they did not expect was the Stillness failing. That failure, not the Root's appetite, is the catastrophe that forced the ritual — and it is why the evacuation is desperate rather than orderly.

Corrach-Nom's founding tale is literally true. The dwarves and gnomes are descendants of the Aftermath — survivors of the last Burial Age who lived in the deep and came up out of it roughly four thousand years ago, into a surface that the ash had just emptied. That is why they got a continent's interior: there was nobody left on it to object. They no longer know this. Four thousand years reduced it to folklore, and the Diarchy's own historians treat the tale as myth.

The Diarchy's foreign delegations are officially Sounding Line scholars operating under Corrach-Nom's authority, and most of them genuinely are — small groups, competent, keeping up appearances against a seat the Diarchy bought and intends to be seen using. Separately and quietly, Corrach-Nom has assembled a unit of its best engineers, foremen and scholars to work the three Undertears in its own territory, looking for recoverable technique rather than knowledge for its own sake. Nothing they have found yet has told them why they find it so legible. Reports from the foreign delegations are read by this unit, which means an ordinary Diarchy scholar at a distant Undertear can be entirely honest and still be the first link in a chain he knows nothing about.

Something else — separate from the insectoids, indifferent to the surface entirely — resides at or near the deepest reachable point of the world, quietly draining warmth out of the depths below the Mid Reaches. What it actually is remains undetermined, even by those piecing the mystery together. GMs evaluating a connection between this presence and the insectoids' failing protection should see Proposed Canon — the link is plausible but not yet confirmed.

The deep world does not simply get hotter with depth, as anyone familiar with mines or wells would expect. Temperature climbs at roughly 2.5–3 °C per kilometre, tops out somewhere near 50–60 °C around twenty kilometres down, and then reverses, growing colder the rest of the way. The first few kilometres are additionally heat-loaded by what the shafts vent upward, which is why shallow mines and wells read reassuringly ordinary and nobody has any reason to look further. Nobody has measured the inversion. The anomaly has gone entirely undetected.

Velkarr is red because it is volcanic. It is locked in a two-to-one resonance with Aumbris, and the tug-of-war between them flexes its interior and keeps it molten; what reaches its surface is sulfur, which is why the colour runs orange through to a near-black red, why the face is repainted every few years, and why the spikes seen at crescent are real — they are eruption plumes hundreds of kilometres tall. None of this is connected to the Burial Ages, to the ash seasons, or to the Root. The world is volcanically violent for its own reasons and so is its inner moon; the resemblance is a coincidence, and a tempting one for any scholar who wants to build a wrong theory on it. No instrument on the surface can resolve any of it, and the resonance itself contributes no meaningful heat to the world below.

The Burial Ages

The Root's draw is not continuous. It is episodic, on a period of roughly four thousand years, and every episode is written into the rock as a Burial Age. When a draw begins in earnest, the heat it displaces drives sustained volcanism at the surface, and the ash that follows ends whatever civilization is standing — not by violence but by years of failed harvests. Ash-loaded rain then seals the abandoned cities fast enough to preserve what was written in them, which is why the deep holds legible ruins rather than rubble.

A Burial Age is not a season of bad weather. It is sustained flood-scale volcanism running for decades to centuries after the last harvest fails — long after there is anyone left to bury. That is what lays down the Floor: primary ashfall at the arcs, then lahars, wind-blown loess, river sediment and slope collapse reworking it into the basins for millennia afterward. Hundreds of metres of accumulated cover is the normal outcome, not an extreme one.

The continents do not gain elevation across cycles, because the crust sinks under the load and cooling lithosphere subsides on its own. Basins keep sinking as they fill; highlands are stripped by erosion instead. Net elevation stays roughly stable, which is why the surface still looks like a surface and not a plateau of stacked ash.

This is also why the deep is stratified. The Undermarch lies beneath kilometres of accumulated Burial Ages, each cycle a few hundred metres, each sealing what the one before it left. The Era Stack is that accumulation read from below.

The current draw is real but early. The aggressive phase is still roughly one to two centuries away, so nothing at the surface will look like a Burial Age beginning within the campaign. The ash seasons everyone already lives with are the shallow end of the same process, and no one has made the connection.

Burial Ages do not close the ways between surface and deep. Whatever sealed them originally was a separate act.

Nobody on the surface knows any of this. The Burial Ages are not suppressed knowledge or contested theory — they are simply gone. Every civilization that could have recorded one was ended by it, and four thousand years reduced whatever survived to flood-and-fire folklore. The physical evidence is in plain sight, in every gorge and sea cliff and now in the upper wall of every Throat, and it means nothing to anyone who looks at it. Long-lived elves may hold fragments closer to the truth than most; nothing in Brannock has been examined on the point.

Peoples who predate the Weeping and still live in the deep keep time by the blooming cycles of a light-emitting fungus, not by any surface calendar. No colonist knows this system exists yet.

Why the ancient ways between surface and deep were originally sealed remains undetermined — a mystery reserved for later, tracked on Open Questions.
