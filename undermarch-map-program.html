Draw These, In This Order

Do not start with the world map. It's the least useful thing to draw right now — you don't yet know what needs to be on it.
#MapWhy now
01Cross-section of the FallwaterYour setting's defining property is verticality. Forces you to answer depth, width, descent method, light, and where the river falls. Best table prop you'll ever make.
02The Lip settlementIsometric or sectional — winch-towers and scaffold clinging to the rim. Players spend every non-delving hour here.
03The local reach — node mapCircles and lines, not accurate cave cartography. Nobody navigates a real cave map at the table, they navigate a route.
04One dungeon site, in fullA single ruin, gridded, ready to run. Just one — prove the pipeline before scaling it.
05The world schematic. Last.Continents in rough mass, twenty Tears marked, watersheds indicated. Resist detail.

Map 1 Brief — Pulled Straight From Canon

ElementWhat's already decided
The riverFalls from the surface through the Throat — your central visual
The Drowned ChapelWedged in the Throat wall, ~40m below the Lip — mark it
The winch-towerThe Compact's, at the Lip, controlling descent
Draft directionResolved: the Fallwater exhales — mist and fog gather near the top, not the bottom
The shaftClean and largely uninterrupted top to bottom — no Shelf, no widening. Mouths are the only breaks, where the Throat happens to intersect a pre-existing cave system
LightReal sun down to the Threshold floor, doline-style pockets of green wherever a ledge catches it
The VergeThe edge of anything surveyed — mark it, and mark that everything below is blank

Real-world reference: mine shaft diagrams, karst cross-sections, cenote profiles, oil rig cutaways. In cave-survey vocabulary this is a projected elevation or profile — the vertical companion to a plan view.

Analog Kit

  • Isometric grid paper — the unlock for cross-sections and scaffold-towns.
  • Dot grid for dungeon interiors; less visually noisy than square grid.
  • Fineliners in three weights (005 / 01 / 03) — hairlines for hatching, medium for walls, heavy for the frame.
  • Tracing / layout paper — draw the rock once, overlay different states.
  • Hachure marks — the standard convention for pit edges and drop-offs.

Tutorials & Communities

For Map 1 — cross-sections and cutaway technique

  • Dyson Logos (dysonlogos.blog) — the reference for hand-drawn dungeon cartography; his cross-hatching technique reads as "cutaway," not "wall."
  • Fantastic Maps (fantasticmaps.com) — pairs a plan view with a profile/elevation view for multi-level caves.
  • 2-Minute Tabletop, "Let's Draw Caves" (YouTube) — real-time cave-wall cross-section demo.

For Map 2 — the isometric Lip settlement

  • Paths Peculiar — "How to Draw an Isometric Dungeon Map" (wistedt.net).
  • Map Effects (mapeffects.co) — isometric fortress tutorial plus a free printable grid.
  • grid.chuhlomin.com — free browser isometric grid generator, no signup.

Communities

  • Cartographers' Guild (cartographersguild.com) — the long-running hub; post WIP threads for critique.
  • r/mapmaking and r/dndmaps — faster feedback.

Real cave-survey vocabulary

  • UIS Basic Cave Mapping Symbols (uisic.uis-speleo.org).
  • NSS Standard Cave Map Symbols (PDF via sacs.caves.org).
  • Plan (top-down) vs. profile/elevation (cross-section) vs. projected/extended profile — knowing the terms improves further searches.

Real-World Reference

  • Hang Son Doong, Vietnam — collapsed ceilings, sunlight, jungle inside the cave.
  • Movile Cave, Romania — sealed 5.5M years, chemosynthetic, no light.
  • Naica, Mexico — crystal caves, lethal heat.
  • Krubera / Veryovkina, Georgia — deepest caves on Earth; study the survey diagrams.
  • Derinkuyu, Turkey — multi-level underground city, real, inhabited.
  • Mammoth Cave, USA — scale and survey conventions.
