# The Undermarch — Player Briefing

*Everything below is common knowledge. Any literate person in a decent-sized town knows most of it; anyone who follows the news knows all of it. You never need to roll to know any of this.*

---

## The Weeping

Two months ago, without warning, the world opened.

It happened everywhere at once. Enormous vertical shafts tore through farmland, forest, mountain and city, and whatever had been standing on that ground is gone. Nobody knows where it went. Nobody has found it.

The event is called **the Weeping**. Nobody knows what caused it.

The immediate aftermath was water. Rivers near several of the new openings simply turned and drained into the earth. Nations that could dam their watersheds in time did so, at ruinous cost. Nations that couldn't were gutted — and the difference between those two groups is now the single largest fault line in surface politics.

We are two months on. The panic has burned off, and what replaced it is worse in its own way: everyone now understands this is permanent, that the openings are not closing, and that the world has a floor it never knew about.

---

## The Undertears

The shafts themselves are called **Undertears**.

Roughly ten are publicly known, and everyone assumes there are more. An opening in unclaimed wilderness takes time to find, and a government that has one inside its borders has obvious reasons not to announce it. Any number you hear quoted is a floor, not a count.

They are enormous, and they are deep — deeper than anyone has measured. Every Undertear has the same rough shape:

- **The Lip** — the surface rim, where the settlement and its winch-towers sit. There is exactly one settlement per Undertear.
- **The Throat** — the long vertical drop, running down and down without meaningfully interrupting.
- **The Mouths** — points where the falling Throat happens to cut into a pre-existing cave system. Some are big enough to hold a handful of people. None is big enough to be a second town.
- **The Verge** — the edge of whatever's been surveyed. Wherever that happens to stop.

Every Throat carries a permanent wind. Some Undertears exhale warm air upward; others inhale it down. Which one you're standing in determines almost everything about how it feels, what the weather does, and what carries.

**Nobody climbs an Undertear.** The distances make it pointless. Descent is by engineered lift — winch towers, cable hoists, counterweighted platforms, staged transfer stations bolted into the rock. All of it went up inside two months under enormous pressure, and it shows: provisional joins, half-finished stages abandoned where the money ran out, loads carried well above what anyone would call safe. Whoever holds a lift line holds everything below it, and everyone knows it.

**The shaft is lit. Nothing else is.** The Sounding Line's lanterns are bolted down the Throat at intervals, lighting the major routes and marking the way back up toward the surface and sideways toward the nearest Mouth. Step off a marked route, or through a Mouth, and you are past the light.

---

## The Undermarch

The Undertears are the way in. **The Undermarch** is what's down there — a labyrinth beneath the crust so large that nobody has mapped a hundredth of it. People who've lived below long enough to love it call it the Undersky instead.

**It is not one connected cave system.** It's a series of isolated regions, separated by gaps that nothing ordinary crosses — flooded sumps, magma sheets, solid rock. Each is reachable from its own Undertear, and not easily from any other. You cannot walk from one Undertear to the next underground. Nobody has managed it.

**Depth is measured in bands,** loosely and by feel: the **Threshold** near the surface, the **Shallows** below it, the **Mid Reaches** further still. Few living explorers have gone past the Mid Reaches and come back able to describe it. Roughly the top three or four bands are surveyed — call it four or five kilometres. Against the whole depth of the thing, that is the shallowest sliver.

**The light changes as you go.** Real sun near the top. Living bioluminescence in the Shallows. Deeper than that, the ruined lamps of civilizations nobody can name — because there were people down there, long before us, and they are not there now. Deeper still, a darkness that ordinary ways of seeing in the dark simply fail against.

**What actually kills people down there is supply.** Water is not the problem — the Lip usually has a river at it. Food and lamp-fuel are the problem, because nothing grows in the dark and nothing burns down there. Every metre of depth is measured in what you carried down.

Old miners' words for bad air are back in daily use: blackdamp, firedamp, stinkdamp, whitedamp. Narrow unventilated pockets are best avoided. And expeditions that stay below long enough lose track of what day it is, because there is nothing to mark one.

## The Three Powers

Three organisations matter. All three operate at most Undertear settlements — this is not a local situation and there are no local answers.

### The Sounding Line

Scholars, engineers, spelunkers and alchemists, organised to survey the Undermarch: what caused the Weeping, whether it can happen again, and what the Undertears actually connect to.

They are chronically understaffed against the size of the job, so they rarely field their own expeditions past a Verge. They sponsor them instead — buying survey data, specimens and reports from anyone willing to sell. If you come back from the dark with something written down, the Line will pay for it.

**Line cable** is their product in universal use below ground: it does not part under load, and every working hoist in every Undertear runs on it.

**Sounding lanterns** are the other one, and you will see them on every major route without ever owning one. They burn without fuel and indicate the direction of the surface — underground, that second property is worth far more than the light. They are not common and they are not for sale; the enchantment is expensive. Most are fixed in the Undertear shafts, lighting the most-used routes and marking the way to the surface and to the nearest Mouth. The rest go out with crews heading into new territory — not to keep, but to leave behind, so that whatever has been explored is marked for whoever comes next. Taking one down is theft from the faction most likely to buy your work, and it blinds a route other people depend on.

### The Undertear Compact

A multinational agreement placing soldiers from rival nations under joint command at the Undertear frontiers, formed to stop any single power seizing the new openings outright. The nations ruined by the flooding pushed hardest for it; the ones who finished their dams in time signed reluctantly.

Their public position is blunt: the Undertears were *dug*, deliberately, from below — and nobody opens that many doors into someone else's house without intending to walk through them. They are preparing for an invasion. Most member governments think this is alarmist. Most of them signed anyway.

A second objective is pursued less loudly and just as hard: nobody unapproved gets rich off the Undermarch.

In practice the Compact is an uneasy coalition rather than an army. National contingents distrust each other, and home governments issue contradictory orders behind its back.

### The Vigil of the Weeping

An old, fringe religious order with scattered chapter-houses that long predate the event, declared heretical by the mainstream Church, and growing very fast.

They grew because they called it. Vigil texts described the opening — the shafts, the swallowed ground, the depth — before it happened, in terms specific enough that dismissing the match has become awkward. Their doctrine holds that the Weeping was not a catastrophe at all but an announcement: the herald of a god rising from below.

Wherever they hold ground they provide medicine and burial rites, free, to anyone. Their scholars catalogue deep anomalies more thoroughly than anyone else alive, and understand very few of them. Their numbers include a great many people who were not religious at all three months ago.

## The World

**Six continents.** **Kethal** is the largest and is where the Compact was founded.

**Kethal holds five nations,** and almost all of them are multiancestral — mixed populations are the long-standing norm here, not the exception.

**Brannock** is the notable exception: an elven nation, woodland, ancient and desert lines sharing one polity, with all the friction that implies.

**Velmack** is where you are, and where this begins.

**The planet is volcanically violent,** and always has been as far as anyone alive knows. Eruptions are frequent, ash seasons are part of the agricultural calendar, and every child learns which mountains to watch. Nobody connects it to anything.

## Home: the Fallwater

The Undertear you live at. Compact paperwork designates it by number; everyone who actually lives there calls it the Fallwater, after the river pouring into its Throat.

The Fallwater **exhales** — the Throat pushes warm air steadily upward. The river falling into it never reaches the bottom. It breaks apart into mist within the first few hundred metres, gets caught in the rising draft, and comes back up as the permanent fog that sits over the Lip. There is a waterfall at the Fallwater that does not land anywhere. Everyone has stopped finding this strange.

The watershed above it lies inside **Velmack**, and Velmack governs the Fallwater in fact as well as in name: the crown at Xynnar issues the charters, sets the tolls, and has the final say over who goes down. Velmack could have dammed the river. It chose not to — it judged an open Undertear worth more than the farmland below it — and the ruined country downstream knows exactly whose decision that was.

All three factions hold ground: a Sounding Line survey chapter studying the Throat and its anomalies, a Compact garrison holding the Lip and controlling who descends, and a Vigil chapter-house doing medicine and funerals.

**One local hazard worth knowing before you go down.** Throat squalls: sudden violent gusts that strike without warning in the Throat and in any passage connected to its airflow. Colonists will tell you that narrow side-passages are *more* dangerous than the open shaft, not less, and they are right.
